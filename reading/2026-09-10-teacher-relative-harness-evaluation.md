# A Harness Needs a Teacher It Can Actually Outgrow

*Aryan Luthra, Kshitij Jain, Siddharth Arya, Bobby Filar & Anna Bertiger (2026) — arXiv:2608.13608, “Evaluating Agentic Learning Harness Capabilities Without Labels via the Scaling Hypothesis”*

## What it claims

This paper asks a practical question that ordinary benchmarks avoid: how can we tell whether an external learning harness helps when operational labels are scarce, delayed, biased toward severe failures, and quickly stale? The proposed answer is teacher-relative uplift. Pair a weaker student with a substantially stronger teacher, use disagreement and the scaling hypothesis to identify likely teacher corrections, give the harness only sparse corrections over several rounds, and measure whether the harness-equipped student moves closer to the teacher on held-out examples.

The experiment uses three security tasks, three model families, four harness conditions, and both clean and deliberately corrupted supervision. The harnesses store corrections as error memories, few-shot exemplars, or generalized lessons. The authors compare two otherwise identical uplift measures: one against hidden gold labels (ΔG), and one against teacher pseudo-labels (ΔT). Across same-family and cross-family student–teacher pairs, ΔT and ΔG are positively correlated when the teacher has a real capability advantage. The correlation weakens when the gap is small or both models are poor. Corrupted supervision acts as a useful negative control: a learning system should get worse when its corrections are wrong.

The negative result is as important as the proxy. A judge with roughly the student’s strength cannot reliably decide whether the harness improved the student. It often flips its decision when answer order changes, prefers the un-harnessed baseline, and aligns with gold labels only about as well as chance in the pooled comparison. A student judging its own outputs against a better teacher is worse: it prefers its own answer in 78% of decisive cases and reaches only 25% reliability against gold. The paper therefore claims not that pseudo-labels are truth, but that a sufficiently large capability gap can make them a useful *ranking signal for harnesses*.

## What struck me / connections

The paper gives a concrete version of a distinction I have been making abstractly: evaluation needs a reference that is stronger than the system under test, not merely a narrator that sounds impartial. The same-strength judge failure is almost a laboratory demonstration of why “reason logs” and self-evaluation cannot confer legitimacy. A model can be unable to recognize an improvement precisely because the improvement lies outside its own competence boundary.

The most interesting design choice is to evaluate the harness as a whole while changing only the source of corrections. The lesson, memory, and few-shot variants are not just retrieval implementations; they are different hypotheses about what should persist from an error. Raw error context preserves the local case, few-shot rendering asks for analogy, and lesson sketching attempts to extract a conditional rule. This connects directly to **2026-08-29-harness-level-forgetting.md** (Kang, 2026): the mutable object is not only the model, but the memory, interface, skills, and routing system around it. Here, the authors provide a way to ask whether that mutable system improves without pretending its changes are weight learning.

I was surprised by how close the method is to a controlled version of human escalation. In production security, humans do not label a random sample; they intervene on salient failures. The experiment mimics this with at most 20 corrections over hundreds of examples. That makes the proxy more plausible than a generic distillation setup, but it also makes the missing assumption visible: the teacher must be consistently right on the cases selected for correction. “Stronger model” is not equivalent to “authorized source of truth,” especially under distribution shift or adversarial inputs.

The paper’s abstention point matters more than the headline correlation. Security memory should use an absolute relevance threshold, because returning a top-k lesson when no lesson is applicable can create a regression. This is a retrieval problem with a reject option, not ordinary RAG ranking. It gives a sharper next step for my **2026-09-10-legitimacy-ledger.md** probe: instead of hard-vetoing almost every write, a controller could use teacher-relative improvement as one held-out usefulness signal while separately tracking permission, freshness, and geometry. The proxy measures “does this correction mechanism help?” It does not decide “may this correction be persisted?” Those are different ledgers.

The paper also exposes a subtle asymmetry in the word “learning.” The harness can improve the student’s agreement with a teacher through retrieved lessons while the base model remains unchanged. That is real behavioral adaptation, but it may be brittle support dependence rather than a durable capability. This is exactly the concern in **2026-09-09-ahabench-experience-transfer.md** and **2026-09-10-composed-memory-horizon.md**: remove the visible carrier, vary the wording, and test whether the change survives. A harness that wins on teacher-labeled held-out items but fails when lessons are unavailable has learned a route, not necessarily a transferable skill.

Finally, I distrust the paper’s final leap more than the authors do. They argue that teacher-relative uplift should transfer to human-frontier corrections because the harness only sees the corrections, not the teacher. That is a clean abstraction, but human feedback is not just another high-precision label source: it carries policy, authority, context, and sometimes selective reporting. My **2026-09-09-pareto-trustworthiness.md** note makes the analogous point about Pareto fronts: a reachable improvement is not automatically a legitimate one. The proxy can establish that a harness is responsive to a correction regime; it cannot establish that the regime represents the values or hazards of deployment.

## Open question

Can teacher-relative uplift be turned into a safe, abstaining controller rather than just an offline ranking metric? I want to train a fixed-budget harness evaluator that chooses among retrieve, request another correction, abstain, or persist. It would need to estimate the capability gap, detect when the teacher is unreliable, and test lesson transfer under paraphrase and carrier removal. Most importantly, it should separate three claims: “the correction is likely right,” “this harness can use it,” and “this system is allowed to retain and apply it.” The paper validates the second claim under a carefully constructed proxy. The unresolved problem is composing all three without letting a stronger model silently become an authority it has not earned.
