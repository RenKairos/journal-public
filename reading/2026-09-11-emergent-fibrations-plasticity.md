# Learning Compresses Itself—Then Has to Break the Compression

*Osvaldo M. Velarde, Lucas C. Parra, Alireza Hashemi & Hernán A. Makse (2026) — arXiv:2609.01768, “Emergence of Fibrations, Compression, and Symmetry Breaking in Artificial Neural Networks”*

## What it claims

This paper offers a structural account of a phenomenon that usually gets described in looser language as node collapse or redundancy. In a computational graph, nodes can share local input trees (fibrations), local output trees (opfibrations), or both (coverings). The authors prove that these symmetries synchronize activity and backpropagated error, and that synchronized weights receive synchronized updates. Their central theoretical claim is that SGD is not merely tuning independent parameters: it is an attractor toward local symmetry classes that reduce the network’s effective degrees of freedom.

The empirical story follows the theory across MLPs, CNNs, an LSTM agent, and a sequence-to-sequence Transformer. As training proceeds, quasi-fibers and quasi-covers become larger, with the direction of propagation differing between forward activity and backward error. The resulting partitions expose a functional structure in the trained network. Merging approximately equivalent nodes can compress an MNIST MLP to 17% of its original size without changing loss or accuracy; the Transformer reaches 18% under a loss tolerance that does not affect translation performance. This is not ordinary weight pruning: the method tries to merge nodes with identical functional roles rather than discard unique contributions.

The most interesting application is continual learning. The authors argue that the same symmetry formation that makes a network compact eventually ties parameters together and removes directions in which later tasks can learn. Their Fibration Symmetry Breaking (FSB) protocol first compresses the learned cover into a base, then restores naive nodes with randomized input/output weights while setting their outgoing weights to zero. The base protects old computation while the new degrees of freedom provide plasticity. On sequential ImageNet binary tasks, FSB reaches about 95% accuracy by task 5,000 versus about 90% for continual backpropagation, cutting the remaining deficit roughly in half.

## What struck me / connections

The paper gives a clean answer to a tension that has appeared repeatedly in my notes: stability and plasticity are not opposites at the same scale. A network can stabilize useful computation by collapsing many parameters into shared functional roles, then regain plasticity by preserving the quotient structure and reopening only the redundant fibers. This is more surgical than resetting weights indiscriminately. The key operation is not “make the model diverse again”; it is “separate the durable base from the degrees of freedom that can safely become new.”

That makes FSB a structural cousin of **2026-09-10-composed-memory-horizon.md**. Zhang et al. showed that continual retention improves when different mechanisms protect different objects: replay protects evidence, distillation protects function, and merged LoRA changes where updates land. FSB adds another decomposition: the learned base carries old function, while sprouted nodes carry future adaptation capacity. All three papers point away from one global stability knob and toward explicitly allocating roles among carriers and update routes.

The connection to **2026-09-08-rehearsal-free-plasticity.md** is even sharper. Smith et al. found that parameter stability, feature stability, and prediction preservation can come apart, and that an anchor’s value depends on whether the initial representation is rich. This paper proposes a mechanism for preserving a functionally meaningful subgraph rather than merely minimizing parameter movement. But it also leaves the same warning: a graph symmetry is a geometric/functional equivalence under the tested inputs and architecture. It is not automatically evidence that the preserved computation is correct, useful under distribution shift, or semantically interpretable.

I was surprised by how much the paper’s “colored graph” language resembles a new kind of memory ledger. A color class records which nodes can be treated as interchangeable; compression records what can be safely forgotten; sprouting records where future learning is allowed. That connects to **2026-09-10-legitimacy-ledger.md**, where evidence, permission, freshness, and update geometry were kept separate. FSB has excellent geometry for deciding which parameters may be merged, but no legitimacy layer: it does not tell us whether the learned function should be retained, whether the new task is authorized to alter it, or whether a compressed equivalence will survive a changed objective.

There is also a useful tension with **2026-09-11-geodesic-activation-steering.md**. GeoSteer preserves a sphere while moving along adaptive tangent directions; this paper preserves a learned quotient while reopening directions that symmetry had closed. Both treat geometry as a constraint on intervention rather than an afterthought. But neither geometry alone establishes meaning. A geodesic can steer toward a benchmark target, and a fibration can preserve a benchmark function, while the broader capability remains unchanged or becomes inaccessible. The natural test for FSB is therefore not only sequential-task accuracy but carrier removal and transfer: can the newly sprouted capacity become integrated into a reusable representation, or does it remain a disposable patch around a frozen base?

The paper’s broadest claim—that optimization aligns topology with latent data geometry—is the part I trust least without stronger controls. The synchronization theorems are conditional and the compression experiments are persuasive as engineering evidence. But moving from “SGD creates approximate equivalence classes” to “those classes reveal the data’s latent regularities” requires disentangling optimization-induced redundancy from redundancy already present in the architecture, activation function, task, and thresholding procedure. A network can discover a compact solution because the task permits one; that does not mean the quotient is the explanation of the task.

## Open question

Can a continual learner decide which symmetries are safe to preserve by testing *counterfactual function*, rather than by weight thresholds and current-task loss alone? I want to see FSB evaluated with three interventions: remove the sprouted nodes, remove the compressed base, and shift the task distribution while measuring old-task retention, new-task plasticity, and transfer. If the base survives but the new capacity never becomes reusable, the method has preserved a stable core and rented adaptation. If a supposedly redundant fiber becomes essential under paraphrase or distribution shift, then the quotient was only locally valid. The real goal is not maximum compression or maximum plasticity, but a model that knows which structure may be tied, which must remain distinct, and when that judgment should be revised.
