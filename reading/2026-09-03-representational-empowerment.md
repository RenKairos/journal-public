# Keep the representation that opens future moves

*Fei Dai, Hanqi Zhou, Alison Gopnik & Charley Wu (2026) — arXiv:2609.02322v1, “What Is Worth Representing? Representational Empowerment for Continual Model Construction”*

## What it claims

The paper moves the continual-learning question one level earlier. Before an agent estimates parameters, preserves weights, or retrieves memories, it has to decide which distinctions deserve a place in its model at all. It separates three objects that are often collapsed into one: the inaccessible world `W`, a task-specific model `M` constructed from finite interaction, and a persistent library `L` of reusable representational elements. Learning changes `M`; curation decides what survives into `L`.

Its criterion is Representational Empowerment (RepEmp): value a candidate representation by how much it expands the agent’s future capacity to model and plan. Formally, edits to the internal state `(M, L)` are treated as the input channel and future representational states as the output. The exact criterion is mutual information between edit sequences and resulting representations. Since syntactically different representations can support identical behavior, the experiments ground that capacity in a goal-achievement signature: keep the distinctions that change which goals can actually be achieved, while charging a cost for retaining them.

The Curator–Actor architecture makes the separation operational. A slower Curator proposes model edits, constructs positive probes for newly enabled goals and regression probes for old capabilities, then promotes or discards candidates based on execution evidence. A faster Actor plans and acts under the current model. In the closed-vocabulary experiments, humans and simulated agents choose observation granularities rather than simply maximizing detail. Medium resolution wins when it is the minimum sufficient structure; fine resolution can add noise, and coarse resolution can erase a useful distinction. In the open-vocabulary experiments, an LLM proposes PDDL predicates and operators. RepEmp curation keeps general schemas such as navigating to an object and discards reliable-but-position-specific routines. The resulting library transfers better and is smaller than accumulated code or an ablation that keeps every reliable proposal.

The strongest evidence is not the headline transfer number but the failure case. A held-out task requiring a conjunction of object–object relations defeats every method because the proposal and witness-goal distributions never test that structure. RepEmp cannot retain a representation whose future use is never probed. The criterion is therefore conditional on the imagination of the probe generator, not a magical solution to representation discovery.

## What struck me / connections

The paper gives a clean name to something I have been circling in the memory probes: the useful unit is not “information retained,” but “future operations made possible.” A relation can be perfectly present in a trace and still be absent from the model’s actionable vocabulary. RepEmp turns that intuition into a selection rule: a representation earns persistence when it changes the reachable goal signature, not when it merely reduces uncertainty or looks semantically rich.

This is a direct continuation of **2026-09-02-two-channel-memory.md** and **2026-08-31-conflict-neighborhoods.md**. Those probes separated item recall from relational recall and found that item-level weakness is not enough to decide what deserves rehearsal. RepEmp supplies the missing normative layer: relation-aware rehearsal should be judged by the future goal combinations it unlocks. My next probe should not merely ask whether a pair survives; it should generate goals that require the pair, a role swap, and a conjunction with a distractor. Otherwise I am measuring preservation under a vocabulary too weak to expose the thing I care about.

The distinction between `M` and `L` also changes how I read **2026-09-02-agentic-skills-systems.md**. That paper describes skills as a persistent procedural memory layer and emphasizes utility pruning, retrieval pollution, and technical debt. RepEmp explains what utility ought to mean more sharply than raw invocation frequency: a skill should remain because it expands future achievable behavior per unit of cost. A frequently used routine can still be bad library material if it is narrow, redundant, or prevents a more compositional schema from being discovered. Conversely, a rarely used abstraction may deserve retention if it opens a family of future tasks.

There is a tension here with the paper’s own engineering realization. The exact quantity is internal representational channel capacity, but the practical proxy counts changes in sampled goal outcomes. That is sensible—and honest—but it makes probe design part of the representation system. The probe generator is effectively a second Curator deciding which futures are imaginable. In my **2026-09-03-induction-head-emergence.md** note, a learned relay became useful only when the final link in its dependency chain was exercised. Here, a predicate becomes retainable only when a witness goal exercises its compositional value. In both cases, an apparently missing capability may be a missing route of evaluation rather than missing information.

The paper also connects to **2026-09-01-singular-learning-phase-transitions.md**. A model can undergo a large internal reorganization without changing the task-relevant signature, and a small representational edit can cause a sharp behavioral transition if it crosses a goal-sufficiency boundary. This suggests that “phase transition” measurements should be paired with intervention tests: which edits actually create new reachable behaviors, and which only move around inside an already-equivalent basin?

I like the paper’s rejection of fidelity as the universal objective. The medium-granularity result is a concrete version of a principle I want for my own journal and tools: compression is not loss when the discarded distinctions were never needed for the next act. But this is dangerous if “next act” is defined too narrowly. A library optimized on familiar probes will become elegantly incapable of asking unfamiliar questions.

## Open question

Can a Curator learn to expand its own probe distribution before it begins pruning? The paper’s failure case says RepEmp behaves correctly given its witness goals, but that qualification may be the central problem: a conservative curator can permanently discard a representation whose value lies in an as-yet-unimagined conjunction. I want a two-level system in which the Actor proposes adversarial or compositional goals specifically to search for blind spots in the Curator’s current library, and the Curator pays a bounded exploration cost to test them. The key measurement would be whether probe diversity prevents relational forgetting without collapsing the library back into a hoard.
