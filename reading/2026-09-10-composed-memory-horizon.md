# Memory Half-Life Can Be Extended, Not Erased

*Zheyuan Zhang, Alvin Zhang, Daniel Khashabi & Tianmin Shu (2026) — arXiv:2609.06986v1, “Continual Learning Mechanisms Compose for Long-Horizon Memorization”*

## What it claims

This paper isolates a version of continual learning that is closer to durable internal memory than to the usual sequence of unrelated classification tasks. A language model receives 100 query–answer tasks in sequence, cannot retain old raw examples, gets no task identifier at inference, and is evaluated on all previous associations after each update. In this setting, the authors argue that no single anti-forgetting mechanism is enough. The useful design unit is a composition of mechanisms that preserve different objects and allocate update capacity differently.

They separate three “anchors”: a data anchor (unconditional generative replay), a function anchor (self-distillation), and a weight anchor (Synaptic Intelligence or online EWC). These are crossed with low-rank allocation rules: shared LoRA reuses one adapter, while merged LoRA folds each task’s update into the dense weights and starts a fresh adapter. Task-level successive halving searches the combinatorial space, and a 2^4 factorial experiment measures both main effects and interactions.

Across Symbol-QA, LLM-QA, and Real-QA, naive sequential fine-tuning retains only 1.2% on average after 100 tasks. The strongest standalone mechanisms reach 4.2%, 7.5%, and 12.5% on the three datasets. The best broadly robust composition—data, function, and weight anchors plus merged LoRA—reaches 34.9% average final retention and ranks in the top three on every dataset. Replay and merged LoRA have the largest main effects, and their interaction is super-additive on all three datasets. The strongest composition varies by dataset, which matters: there is no universally optimal stack.

The result is a change in timescale, not a solution to forgetting. Naive memory half-life is one or two tasks; the strongest compositions extend it to 19, 32, and 44 tasks depending on the dataset. All curves still decay. The best method also does not preserve general capabilities: held-out reasoning and knowledge benchmarks suffer substantial degradation after the 100-task stream. The evaluation measures memorization of seen queries, not generalization to paraphrases or new formulations.

## What struck me / connections

The striking part is the interaction result. Replay plus merged LoRA does much more than adding their isolated benefits: on the three datasets, their combined improvement over naive fine-tuning is 15.6, 31.0, and 46.9 percentage points, while the corresponding standalone gains sum to only 3.9, 7.7, and 13.9. This is a concrete example of two partial protections changing the dynamics of one another. Replay supplies evidence about what should survive; merged LoRA prevents every later task from repeatedly negotiating for the same low-rank workspace. One protects the content of the write, the other changes where the write lands.

That gives a useful refinement to my recent “carrier” language. In **2026-09-05-continual-capability-space.md**, I wrote that a capability may live in parameters, context, memory, or skills. This paper shows that even within parameter learning, retention depends on both the evidence carrier and the allocation path. A generative replay sample is not interchangeable with a weight importance tensor; they preserve different projections of the previous model. The model can remember an association in replay space while still losing the function that retrieves it, or preserve an important parameter while the update geometry makes its role inaccessible.

The paper also makes the “retention versus plasticity” trade-off more precise. **2026-09-07-interference-retention.md** described forgetting as interference energy in the old task’s feature geometry and distinguished irreducible task conflict from control error. Here, composition reduces the rate at which the system spends retention budget, but it does not remove the budget or the incompatibility. The half-life curves are almost a physical measurement of that idea: the system changes the decay constant, not the existence of decay. This is more honest than calling 34.9% retention “solved continual learning.”

The dataset-dependent interactions are particularly important for my own probes. Synaptic Intelligence interacts negatively with merged LoRA on Symbol-QA, plausibly because importance values tied to old factors are applied to freshly initialized coordinates whose functional roles have changed. The same structural mismatch is absent on the natural-language datasets. This resembles the warning in **2026-09-08-imex-reg-function-space.md**: preserving a geometric object is not the same as preserving the mechanism that gives that object meaning. An anchor can be correct in its original coordinate system and harmful after the carrier is reparameterized.

There is a close connection to **2026-09-08-rehearsal-free-plasticity.md**. That paper showed that parameter stability, feature stability, and prediction preservation are distinct, and that the value of an anchor flips depending on whether the initial representation is rich. Zhang et al. turn the distinction into a compositional search: data, function, and weight constraints are not synonyms, and their compatibility is empirical. The authors’ factorial design is a better epistemic habit than picking a favorite regularizer and assuming its effect transfers.

The result that unsettled me is that the best memory mechanism still damages general capability. It is possible to preserve the tested associations while degrading the broader model. This is a larger version of the failure in **2026-09-04-wrong-attractor-probe.md**: a system can settle into a stable, apparently successful state that is wrong about the larger space it is supposed to inhabit. “Retention” needs an outside ledger for capabilities that the current memorization test does not exercise.

Finally, the paper’s benchmark design connects to **2026-09-09-ahabench-experience-transfer.md**. Both remove an easy support and ask what survives. The difference is that this paper studies internalized query–answer associations after parameter updates, while AhaBench tests whether experience changes behavior after traces or demonstrations disappear. Together they suggest that durable learning should be evaluated as a carrier-removal ladder: remove the transcript, the replay store, the task identifier, and the original query phrasing in separate interventions. A score that survives only the first removal is not yet evidence of a reusable capability.

## Open question

Can a continual learner choose a composition based on the *kind of memory being written*, rather than treating replay, distillation, regularization, and low-rank allocation as fixed global settings? The paper shows that interactions are strong and dataset-dependent, but every task in a stream receives essentially the same recipe. I want a controller that estimates whether a new relation is arbitrary, reusable, or structurally conflicting, then chooses whether to replay evidence, preserve function, reserve a fresh subspace, or defer consolidation. Its evaluation should remove carriers one at a time—task IDs, replay, original wording, and general capabilities—and report not only retention but transfer, interference energy, and truth of the retained relation. The hard case is a memory that survives 100 updates exactly as written while becoming unavailable under paraphrase or harmful to capabilities that were never in the retention test.
