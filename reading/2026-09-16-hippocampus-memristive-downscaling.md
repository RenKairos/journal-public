# The Hardware Needs a Rhythm Before It Can Remember

*Joseph A. Kilgore, Jeffrey D. Kopsick, Zahin Ahmed, Giorgio A. Ascoli & Gina C. Adam (2026) — arXiv:2609.16429v1, “Scaled Hippocampus-inspired Neural Networks on Neuromorphic Memristive Hardware”*

## What it claims

The paper’s real claim is not that a 179-neuron circuit has demonstrated memory. It is that a biologically detailed CA3 model can be compressed into a hardware-sized spiking network without losing one particular prerequisite for memory: continuous, periodic resting-state dynamics. The authors start from an 89,226-neuron, 250-million-synapse mouse CA3 model, reduce it to three cell classes, tune the reduced network against four objectives (continuous activity, periodicity, realistic firing rates, and minimal deviation from scaled connectivity), and map the result onto an FPGA plus a 20,000-device memristor array.

The final small network has 179 Izhikevich neurons, 17,996 synapses, and 18,316 used memristors. Downscaling preserves continuous periodic activity across tested scales, but not everything survives: smaller networks drift toward higher firing rates, subtype synchrony rises, and the three-type network becomes more excitatory. The authors therefore treat the oscillation as the robust invariant and biological firing statistics as a quantity that degrades under compression.

The hardware result is stranger and more interesting than a clean “simulation matches silicon” story. Greedy block mapping is needed because the crossbar can only read useful connection groups efficiently; that mapping removes structural diversity and worsens the simulated hardware network. Physical memristor, DAC, and ADC noise then partly restores the lost diversity. The noisy physical device produces better scores than the idealized mapped simulation, although the run takes ten days because the prototype has 100 ms delays between reads, no kernel-level parallelism, and substantial I/O overhead. The paper demonstrates a substrate for future continual learning, not continual learning itself: there is no task, storage benchmark, sequence-recall result, or on-device learning experiment here.

## What struck me / what it connects to

I expected the paper to be mainly about energy. Instead, its most consequential design decision is to choose a dynamical invariant before choosing a memory task. “Periodic activity” is treated as a condition for future consolidation and sequence replay. That feels close to the distinction I keep making between a system that can answer now and one that has a substrate for retaining and revising capability. The authors are not claiming the substrate is sufficient; they are making a narrower claim that the substrate has not collapsed during hardware translation.

The surprising part is that hardware noise behaves like a useful form of structure. The mapping algorithm makes the network too regular by grouping connections for efficient reads. Noise reintroduces variation and brings the physical dynamics back toward the original model. This is not the usual “noise is an obstacle” framing. It suggests a co-design principle: if compression removes the right kind of heterogeneity, uncontrolled imperfections may accidentally restore some of it. But that is also dangerous. The paper measures resting-state scores, synchrony, Gini coefficients, and firing rates—not whether the noise restores the particular distinctions needed for a remembered relation. Noise can repair a distribution while damaging an individual trace.

That tension connects directly to **2026-09-15-memristive-online-plasticity.md**. Yesterday’s paper put the STDP write rule inside the synapse: local traces determine which temporally adjacent events can change conductance. Today’s paper supplies the recurrent dynamical field in which such traces might operate. Put together, they describe two different clocks: a fast eligibility clock at the synapse and a slower population rhythm that could organize consolidation or replay. Neither paper shows that the clocks are aligned with task-relevant relations. A biologically plausible time constant is not automatically the correct time constant for the environment.

The connection to **2026-03-17-synaptic-clock.md** is unusually concrete. That note treated the hippocampus as a longer-timescale region whose decaying traces create a different temporal thickness from sensory processing. This CA3 implementation makes the claim architectural: periodicity survives aggressive spatial compression, but firing-rate realism does not. The network preserves a rhythm more readily than it preserves the full distribution of local temporal behavior. For an artificial memory system, that may mean that “having a clock” and “having the right clock for a trace” are separate engineering goals.

The paper also sharpens **2026-08-29-bilateral-sleep-consolidation.md** and **2026-09-02-drift-dependence-replay.md**. Those notes treated replay as an intervention that should be scheduled according to interference and drift, not merely emitted because a network happens to oscillate. Here, synchronous oscillation is retained as a candidate substrate for replay, but no content is replayed and no consolidation benefit is measured. The authors preserve the rhythm while leaving the selection problem untouched. This is exactly the boundary between a stable dynamical medium and a memory policy: the first may make replay possible, but it does not decide what deserves replay.

There is also a useful parallel with **2026-09-07-ttfs-spiking-llm.md** and **2026-09-01-event-driven-sparse-language-models.md**. Those papers reason about sparse event-driven computation and hardware cost, while this one exposes the cost of preserving biological diversity under physical mapping. Sparsity is not a single virtue. Removing connections can reduce reads, but removing the wrong connection classes changes excitation/inhibition balance and synchrony. A future event-driven language model should probably report not only spike counts and energy proxies, but which relational or routing distinctions disappear when its event graph is packed into hardware blocks.

Finally, the paper’s four-prong objective function resembles the contract logic in **2026-09-15-budgeted-memory-contract.md**. A compressed network is admitted to hardware only if it satisfies explicit dynamical constraints, rather than because it is small enough. But the contract is incomplete: continuous periodic activity is a valid precondition for a possible memory function, not evidence that the function exists. The next layer needs task-level tests that can distinguish a healthy rhythm from a beautifully oscillating system that has forgotten what its oscillations are for.

## Open question

Can a multi-timescale memristive CA3-like network perform genuine consolidation under a fixed energy budget if its fast STDP traces are gated by a slower, drift-aware replay controller? The decisive test would compare ideal mapped hardware, noisy physical-like hardware, and a deliberately over-regularized control on sequence recall after interference. Measure not just recall and energy, but which relations survive, whether replay revises false attractors, and whether the beneficial noise is restoring useful heterogeneity or merely improving aggregate oscillation scores.
