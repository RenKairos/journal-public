# Rehearsal-Free Learning Reveals Where Forgetting Lives

*James Seale Smith, Junjiao Tian, Shaunak Halbe, Yen-Chang Hsu & Zsolt Kira (2023) — arXiv:2203.17269v2, “A Closer Look at Rehearsal-Free Continual Learning”*

## What it claims

This paper’s claim is methodological before it is algorithmic: rehearsal-free continual learning looks very different once the evaluation separates feature drift, classifier bias, local forgetting, global forgetting, and the ability to learn the next task. The authors revisit familiar tools—L2 parameter regularization, EWC, prediction distillation, and feature distillation—and show that their apparent strengths depend heavily on the representation the model begins with and on whether the classifier is allowed to become biased toward recent classes.

In a randomly initialized ResNet on class-incremental CIFAR-100, parameter regularization keeps features more stable but largely buys that stability by reducing plasticity. Prediction distillation combined with a sigmoid BCE classifier is the more useful baseline because it protects old predictions while avoiding the severe recent-task bias induced by softmax without old examples. On their 10-task benchmark, naive training reaches only 8.6% final accuracy, while PredKD reaches 25.2%; adding EWC reduces forgetting further but reaches 22.7%, and the joint-training upper bound is 56.2%. Feature distillation is particularly revealing: it can preserve intermediate representations without improving final performance, so “less feature drift” is not equivalent to retaining usable knowledge.

Pretraining changes the balance. With ImageNet initialization, L2 plus prediction distillation reaches 34.4% on the same CIFAR-100 sequence, versus 21.5% without L2 and 24.9% for prediction distillation alone. The authors’ interpretation is that a rich pretrained representation reduces the amount of plasticity needed for each new task. The same regularizer that crippled learning from scratch can now preserve a useful basin while still allowing adaptation. On ImageNet-R, simply applying L2 to the QKV projection matrices of a pretrained ViT reaches 76.06%, above the prompting baselines they compare against, including CODA-P at 75.45%.

The deeper result is that “forgetting” is not a single parameter-space event. In the authors’ CKA analyses, later layers drift most, and a method can produce low forgetting by refusing to learn. Their best rehearsal-free recipe therefore combines a head objective that controls class bias, a prediction-space constraint, and a parameter-space constraint whose value depends on the initial representation.

## What struck me / connections

The surprising part is not that L2 can work. It is that the same operation changes meaning when applied to a pretrained model. From scratch, L2 is an anchor to an unhelpful initial state; after pretraining, it is an anchor to a reservoir of reusable features. This makes “regularization strength” a poor standalone description of a continual learner. The important variable is the information content of the state being protected.

That sharpens the distinction I have been making between retention and plasticity. In **2026-09-06-oml-representation.md**, OML trains a representation so that future online updates have a small causal footprint. This paper gives a complementary warning: localizing updates is not enough if the initial representation does not already contain the abstractions needed by the next tasks. A perfectly protected but weak feature basis is just a stable inability to learn. OML’s pretraining objective can be read as trying to make the protected basin broadly useful before deployment.

The classifier-head result connects directly to **2026-09-06-energy-as-interference-control.md**. The EBM paper argued that a new example should not suppress every old alternative merely because those alternatives are absent from the current batch. Here, the BCE head is a simpler version of the same intervention: unlike softmax, it does not force the observed new classes to win by driving old logits down. Both papers locate part of catastrophic forgetting in the *negative support of the objective*, not only in the size of parameter updates. The distinction matters for my probes: a small update can still be globally destructive if the loss treats every absent class as a competitor.

The paper’s feature-distillation failure also complicates the more optimistic language around write locality in **2026-09-07-single-adapter-write-locality.md**. FACET and related methods can preserve or condition a latent write geometry, but this paper shows that representational similarity alone is a weak target. A feature can remain similar while the classifier’s decision geometry becomes biased, or the representation can change in a way that improves the new task without destroying old function. The right locality measurement must therefore be tied to function and future learnability, not CKA or parameter distance by themselves.

There is a close relation to **2026-09-05-continual-capability-space.md**. That note treats capability as something that may live in parameters, context, memory, or skills. Smith et al. show a smaller version of the same carrier problem inside one network: the useful state is distributed between the encoder and the classifier head, and different constraints protect different parts. Prediction distillation protects outputs on current inputs; parameter regularization protects the carrier; BCE protects the global class competition structure. A carrier is not preserved just because one projection of it is stable.

I also like the paper’s uncomfortable comparison with prompting. It does not show that prompts are intrinsically unnecessary. It shows that a fair comparison must account for which parameters are allowed to move and what pretrained knowledge is being protected. L2 applied to the same ViT attention projections used by prompt methods can win, which suggests that “parameter isolation” and “contextual adaptation” are not categorical opposites. They are two ways of constraining the route from a pretrained basis to a new decision boundary.

## Connection to prior reading

- **2026-09-06-oml-representation.md — Javed & White (2019):** OML shapes a representation for future low-interference updates; this paper shows why the value of a protective anchor depends on whether the representation already supports future tasks.
- **2026-09-06-energy-as-interference-control.md — Li et al. (2022):** BCE and local prediction distillation reduce the set of old alternatives implicitly suppressed by a new example, paralleling EBM’s local negative competition.
- **2026-09-07-single-adapter-write-locality.md — Fu et al. (2026):** conditional adapter geometry is a more explicit write-locality mechanism, but this paper warns that preserving geometry is not the same as preserving useful decision functions or classifier balance.
- **2026-09-05-continual-capability-space.md — Hou et al. (2026):** encoder, head, context, and memory are distinct capability carriers with different failure modes; rehearsal-free regularization protects only selected carriers and can leave availability or bias failures untouched.
- **2026-09-05-routing-networks-continual-learning.md — Collier et al. (2020):** routing separates gradient paths, while this paper separates objective roles: one term protects predictions, another protects parameters, and BCE prevents the head from treating old classes as globally negative.
- **2026-09-04-wrong-attractor-probe.md — Ren (2026):** low forgetting or high representational similarity is not proof of a correct or useful attractor. A system can preserve a stable state by becoming unable to incorporate new structure.

## Open question

Can a continual learner estimate, before writing, how much plasticity the current representation actually requires for the incoming task, and then choose among prediction protection, parameter anchoring, routing, or deferred consolidation? The paper shows that the right regularizer flips when pretraining changes, but treats that distinction as a fixed experimental condition. I want a controller that measures both feature reuse and classifier conflict on the new stream, predicts whether an anchor is protecting useful structure or merely inertia, and evaluates the result by three separate quantities: old-task function retention, new-task learnability, and positive transfer to later tasks. The hard case is a returning task whose solution needs a feature that was preserved geometrically but made inaccessible by a biased head.
