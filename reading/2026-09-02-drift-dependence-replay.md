# Replay Has Two Failures, Not One

*Tieliang Gong, Zhongbo Zhang, Wen Wen & Yong-Jin Liu (2026) — arXiv:2608.11690v1, “Drift and Dependence: Layer-wise Information-Theoretic Bounds for Replay-Based Continual Learning”*

## What it claims

The paper’s useful move is to refuse the phrase “replay generalization error” as if it named one thing. A finite buffer creates a representation-level approximation error: the stored examples are not merely a smaller dataset, because the feature map was itself shaped by those examples. Reusing the same buffer also creates optimization dependence: old samples, new samples, and the final parameters are tied together by one trajectory. The main theorem separates these as replay-centroid drift and optimization dependence, then splits the latter into stability, plasticity, interaction, and residual coupling.

The decomposition is layer-wise. At the input, the stored samples are i.i.d. proxies and the representation drift vanishes; at the output, there is no trainable suffix left to mediate stability/plasticity, so drift dominates. Between those endpoints, the paper predicts a “generalization funnel”: a layer where representation mismatch is not yet large but the remaining suffix is no longer strong enough to amplify it badly. The proposed stabilization target is therefore an interior region, not automatically the features or the classifier head.

The finite-memory term has a concrete effective sample size, `N_eff = 1 / ((T−1)/m + 1/n)`. This predicts a floor: increasing current-task data cannot eliminate the variance caused by a fixed replay budget, and the relevant total-risk scale grows with the number of old tasks. In the controlled Gaussian experiment, the measured memory exponent is −0.443 (95% CI [−0.498, −0.391]) rather than the ideal −1/2, but the proxy still tracks the gap across the memory/sample grid with Pearson `r=0.98` and `R²=0.97`.

The second operational result replaces KL drift with Wasserstein distance when finite replay support makes KL vacuous. The drift–suffix-sensitivity product has an interior minimum in every one of the 12 deep-network cells tested. The minimizing region moves deeper as the network gets deeper, but it is broad: layers within 20% of the minimum occupy 30–47% of the interior. This is a method for choosing where to stabilize, not evidence for a universal canonical layer.

For the optimization branch, an SGLD instantiation produces a curvature-aware gradient alignment `cos_H`, rather than the ordinary Euclidean gradient cosine used by GEM/OGD. In controlled Rotate/Flip-MNIST, the alignment separates positive transfer from interference more sharply than the Euclidean measure and correlates more strongly with forgetting (`−0.89` versus `−0.84`). On Split-CIFAR-100 and Split-TinyImageNet, the signal remains strong for ER and DER++ (partial correlations from `−0.645` to `−0.948`) but fails for iCaRL, whose distillation and exemplar-classifier dynamics are outside the modeled replay-gradient mixture. That failure is important: the theory identifies its own scope boundary instead of presenting one diagnostic as method-independent.

## What struck me / connections

I came expecting another information-theoretic bound that would be too loose to guide anything. The constants are indeed loose, but the paper’s *factorization* is more actionable than the bound itself. It gives two separate levers: increase memory or reduce representation drift for the compression channel; change update geometry or task mixing for the dependence channel. “Catastrophic forgetting” becomes a diagnosis problem with distinct causes rather than a single score to optimize.

This reframes my recent memory work. **2026-08-31-fast-weight-memory.md** treats a recurrent state as a learner that updates online. Gong et al. expose the same distinction at a different scale: replay is not just a memory store, it is a data-dependent training trajectory. A fast-weight state can therefore fail by forgetting its contents (state dynamics) or by learning an update rule whose new-task gradients systematically damage old-task directions (trajectory dependence). The two failures should not be conflated in a probe.

The layer-wise result connects directly to **2026-08-31-decay-aware-state-quantization.md**. That note asks where precision buys the most retention under recurrent decay. This paper asks where stabilization buys the most generalization under representation drift and suffix amplification. Both imply that “protect the memory” is underspecified: the optimal intervention is located by the product of a local vulnerability and the downstream dynamics that amplify it. For a recurrent system, I would look for an analogous funnel over state coordinates or update stages, not assume the input state or final readout is the right place to spend bits.

The alignment result also gives a sharper interpretation of **2026-08-31-conflict-neighborhoods.md**. That probe found that relation recall improved when rehearsal targeted ablation-instability, even though item recall barely moved. Here, curvature-aware old/new gradient alignment is a signal of whether the next update will damage prior behavior. These are dual notions of instability: one asks which *stored relations* are fragile under removal; the other asks which *task memories* are fragile under an incoming gradient. A combined rehearsal controller could prioritize an example or relation only when it is both structurally indispensable and geometrically threatened.

I also like the paper’s negative result on iCaRL. It blocks an easy but dangerous move in my own experiments: logging a useful statistic under one algorithm and treating it as a law of forgetting. Diagnostics are not properties of models alone; they are properties of a model–update-rule pair. The event-driven sparsity note made a similar hardware point—sparsity only has meaning relative to a machine’s movement primitive. Here, alignment only has meaning relative to the optimizer’s actual coupling mechanism.

## Open question

Can a single online controller estimate both channels cheaply enough to act on them? Concretely: for a bounded recurrent memory, can I estimate (1) relation-level ablation instability and representation drift, and (2) curvature-aware old/new update interference, then choose whether to rehearse, increase precision, or alter the update? The decisive test would hold total memory and compute fixed while comparing a controller that sees only item weakness against one that sees the two-channel decomposition, measuring relational recall, old-task recovery after interference, and adaptation speed separately.
