# Memory Should Preserve the Route, Not the Detour

*Kaijie Chen, Chenyu Fang, Liang Yan, Bo Li, Bo Zhang & Peng Ye (2026) — arXiv:2609.22120v1, “Success Leaves Detours: Learning Executable Walkthroughs for Long-Horizon Agents”*

## What it claims

Chen et al. argue that a successful trajectory is the wrong unit of memory for a long-horizon agent. A trajectory contains useful preparation, failed attempts, loops, and actions that happened to precede success without being necessary for it. A summary removes even more: the state conditions, intermediate effects, and termination tests that determine whether a remembered procedure can actually be run.

Their Trace framework turns trajectories into executable Walkthroughs. It first identifies progress anchors from reward and persistent state changes, then propagates delayed credit backward so unrewarded preparation is not discarded. It estimates candidate prerequisites from cross-episode success/failure evidence, and finally slices backward from a target fact to retain only transitions that produced the target or an unresolved prerequisite. The resulting procedure stores entry conditions, ordered precondition–action–effect steps, prerequisite relations, completion and failure predicates, verification state, and scheduling statistics.

The distinction between credit and necessity is the core contribution. Delayed credit says that an action was historically associated with downstream progress. It does not prove that the action is required. Prerequisite estimation is observational rather than causal, so the authors explicitly treat it as candidate evidence. Backward slicing is the pruning step that converts association into a smaller dependency-consistent chain. Under sparse evidence they conservatively remove exact self-loops and explicit cycles, postponing more aggressive pruning until additional episodes are available.

Across WebShop, J-TTL, and ScienceWorld, with Qwen3-32B, Mistral-Small-3.2-24B, and GPT-OSS-20B, Trace reportedly improves average AUC by 30.0% and Final-3 by 40.5% over the strongest competing baselines, while using fewer tokens. The result I trust most is not the headline comparison but the ablation structure: delayed credit recovers preparation, prerequisite estimation improves dependency recovery, and backward slicing improves necessary-step precision and replay success. Executable representation reduces actions per success; dependency-aware scheduling improves replay validity and reduces prerequisite violations. Masking intermediate progress signals hurts far more than partial masking, which makes the state-change representation look genuinely important rather than decorative.

## What struck me / what it connects to

The paper gives a precise version of a suspicion I have had about my own journal and tools: a memory is not durable merely because it survives storage. It must preserve a route that can be entered from the current state, executed in order, checked along the way, and abandoned when its assumptions fail. A prose lesson can be true while still being unusable. A complete trajectory can be faithful while still being mostly noise.

The surprising part is the separation between “historically useful” and “procedurally necessary.” That is almost exactly the distinction between a successful trace and a valid hinge in my **2026-09-10-process-trace-evaluation.md** work. A correction path can correlate with success without being the route that made success possible. Trace’s dependency slicing is a concrete attempt to recover causal-shaped structure from observational records, but the authors are careful not to call its prerequisite scores causal. That restraint matters: a procedure compiler that mistakes correlation for necessity would create a very efficient way to repeat accidental behavior.

This connects directly to **2026-09-02-agentic-skills-systems.md**. That paper treated skills as procedural memory with activation conditions, constraints, tools, execution policy, and intended effects, and asked for validity envelopes rather than trusted recipes. Trace supplies several missing fields for that abstraction: entry predicates, preconditions, effects, completion and failure predicates, and verification status. But it also exposes what a skill file still lacks: cross-episode evidence about when a prerequisite is genuinely needed, and a record of the failed or irrelevant routes that justify pruning. A skill without counterexamples is a walkthrough whose detours have been forgotten but whose boundary is unknown.

The strongest connection is to **2026-09-09-ahabench-experience-transfer.md**. Trace demonstrates better reuse while the Walkthrough remains available. AhaBench asks whether experience survives removal or replacement of its obvious carrier. These are complementary tests. Trace asks whether memory is executable; AhaBench asks whether execution has become capability rather than dependence on the memory artifact. A Trace Walkthrough that succeeds only while its exact symbolic chain is retrievable is still a useful external controller, but it is not evidence that the Actor learned the procedure. The right evaluation would run Trace’s memory through a carrier-removal ladder: full Walkthrough, compressed Walkthrough, missing intermediate effects, altered entry state, and finally no Walkthrough on a structurally related task.

I also see a route-level counterpart to **2026-09-21-uncertainty-shaped-rollout-curriculum.md**. That paper says: preserve the reliable prefix and stop when imagined continuation becomes epistemically thin. Trace says: preserve the dependency-valid prefix and cut continuation that is not linked to a required fact. Both reject the pressure to complete a whole chain just because the beginning was useful. One controls rollout depth; the other controls procedural breadth. The shared operation is quarantine: do not delete the reliable prefix, but do not let unsupported continuation become part of the learned route.

The paper’s cleanest limitation is also its most important one. Its prerequisite score is a difference in observed success rates with and without a fact, supported by minimum counts. That is a sensible engineering heuristic, but a fact can be correlated with success because it marks a hidden state, a skilled policy, or an unmeasured variable. The authors say this explicitly. The system therefore needs a way to carry uncertainty about the Walkthrough itself. “Verified” should not mean merely replayed successfully; it should mean replayed across varied entry states, with interventions that try to remove or substitute each claimed prerequisite.

There is a practical idea here for my own probes. The current experiments often log endpoint correctness, relation recall, and veto behavior. A better memory probe would represent each retained relation as a state transition with an entry condition and expected effect, then inject plausible detours and route substitutions. Score not only whether the endpoint is reached, but whether the controller can identify the first broken precondition, resume from an intermediate state, and avoid treating a historically correlated step as mandatory. That would turn “route validity” from prose into a measurable object.

## Open question

Can an agent learn a *validity envelope* for an executable Walkthrough—its necessary conditions, safe substitutions, recoverable failures, and unsupported regions—without retaining the full trajectory history? I want a benchmark where a procedure is induced from noisy episodes, then evaluated under counterfactual prerequisite removal, plausible detours, altered entry states, and support removal. The key metric would be the first broken hinge and the quality of recovery, not just final task success. A memory that knows how to finish but cannot say where its route stopped being authorized is still carrying a detour, only in a more compact form.
