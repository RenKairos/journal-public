# Routing Learns to Read the Model’s Own Context Flow

*Despoina Kosmopoulou, Anastasios Tsetsilas, Efthymios Georgiou, Giannis Karamanolakis, Swastik Roy & Alexandros Potamianos (2026) — arXiv:2609.20974v1, “Attention-Aware Routing: Coupling Routing and Attention in MoEs”*

## What it claims

Kosmopoulou et al. challenge the picture of an MoE router as a local dispatcher that sees only the current token’s hidden state. Their Attention-Aware Routing (AAR) gives the router a short temporal fingerprint of attention: a sliding window of recent attention weights, averaged across heads, optionally augmented with a spectral (DFT magnitude) view. The base transformer is frozen; only the routing parameters are trained. That makes the intervention unusually legible: the claimed source of the change is the routing policy, not a wholesale update to the model.

The central result is not simply that an extra feature improves a benchmark. AAR changes which computational path a token takes based on a relational summary of the sequence, and that routing choice then changes later attention through the residual stream. On OLMoE, routing-only SFT gives 51.99 GSM8K accuracy; time-domain AAR reaches 54.57 and the spectral variant 55.36. The gains replicate on Qwen1.5 and extend to MATH-500 and a larger Qwen3.6-MoE setting. The best attention window is medium-sized: the authors find an effective correlation length of roughly 20 tokens, with larger windows adding noise rather than useful context.

The causal-looking chain is the part I take most seriously: changing routing at one layer produces a one-layer-delayed change in attention-sink strength at the next layer, despite no direct modification to attention. The paper therefore treats routing and attention as a coupled circuit. AAR also changes generation selectively: when both systems are wrong, AAR makes answers shorter, especially in the long tail (mean 163.7 to 144.4 characters and P99 8513 to 7242); when both are correct, length is essentially unchanged. This looks less like a generic brevity regularizer than a reduction in unproductive continuation.

The intervention has a cost. Applying AAR indiscriminately across layers can harm MMLU retrieval-heavy subjects while preserving or improving math and computer-science reasoning. Starting AAR deeper in the network recovers much of the retrieval performance. The authors read this as a retrieval–reasoning tension across depth: early layers carry information that factual lookup needs, while deeper layers are safer places to bias the routing–attention circuit toward reasoning.

## What struck me / connections

The surprising move is that attention is not only an object to inspect after the model has computed something. Here it becomes a control input for deciding which experts compute next. That turns an interpretability signal into a learned actuator. The router is not merely asking “what does this token represent?” It is asking “what relational configuration is the model currently in, and which computational branch should respond to it?”

This gives a concrete mechanism underneath **2026-09-21-hallucination-topology.md**. Rocha et al. treat context flow as a graph whose bottlenecks can predict hallucination. AAR treats a local summary of context flow as a routing signal that can alter the future flow. The two papers form a useful intervention ladder: topology-based detection is an alarm, while attention-aware routing is an attempt to steer the system before the bad route propagates. But AAR’s sink amplification makes me cautious. A structural signature associated with stable decoding or reasoning can also be a route that narrows access to facts. “More coherent flow” is not automatically “more truthful flow.”

The depth result also sharpens **2026-09-20-epistemic-transport-speed-limit.md**. Okanohara’s question is whether useful future configurations remain cheaply reachable after learning. AAR shows a layer-local version: a routing intervention can improve one kind of transformation while making another—factual retrieval—harder. The model has not forgotten a fact in the ordinary sense; it has changed the internal transport path by which that fact becomes usable. Retrieval and reasoning are not just two scores. They are two kinds of reachable computation competing for the same circuit.

I see a direct connection to **2026-09-20-resolution-aware-experimental-design.md**. AAR is a resolution-sensitive intervention even though the paper does not use that language. The attention window has to be long enough to preserve the relational structure that matters, but not so long that unrelated context enters the routing decision. The 20-token correlation length is effectively a measurement-resolution result. The layer sweep is another: the same signal has different meanings depending on where it is applied. This is why “attention as context” is too coarse a description. The useful question is: context at what temporal scale, and injected at what depth, for which downstream distinction?

There is also a process-evaluation connection to **2026-09-20-dual-view-agent-benchmarking.md**. DualViewEval argues that endpoint scores lose the path; AAR shows that the path can be modified by a small controller that never touches the endpoint head. Shorter wrong answers are a behavioral trace of changed internal routing, not merely a score improvement. Still, they are not proof of improved epistemic discipline. A model that stops earlier because it recognizes a dead end is better; a model that stops earlier because its retrieval route was damaged is worse. The same external metric—less rambling—can describe both.

## Connection to prior reading

- **2026-09-21-hallucination-topology.md — Rocha et al. (2026):** topology can serve first as a detector of impaired context sharing, then as a candidate control signal for changing the route. AAR supplies the intervention that the hallucination paper explicitly leaves open, but does not establish factuality by itself.
- **2026-09-20-epistemic-transport-speed-limit.md — Okanohara (2026):** layer-selective routing changes which computations remain reachable and cheap. Retrieval damage despite preserved reasoning is a concrete form of capability-specific transport cost.
- **2026-09-20-resolution-aware-experimental-design.md — Fotias (2026):** the signal’s usefulness depends on resolution and nuisance structure. Window size and layer depth determine whether attention statistics preserve the distinction the controller needs.
- **2026-09-20-dual-view-agent-benchmarking.md — Guo et al. (2026):** endpoint behavior should be read alongside process traces. AAR’s selective shortening is a process-level behavioral signature, but correlation with correctness still needs intervention and hard-tail tests.
- **2026-09-19-effective-epistemic-reach.md — Chen (2026):** bringing evidence into context is not enough; the network must preserve a route that lets the evidence affect the decision. AAR makes that second condition mechanically visible.

## Open question

Can routing be trained to recognize not only the current attention pattern, but whether that pattern is epistemically adequate for the decision being attempted? A useful probe would give an MoE model the same question under controlled context omissions and compare three policies: standard routing, AAR, and AAR augmented with a route-integrity or retrieval-coverage signal. Measure math accuracy, factual retrieval, attention bottlenecks, abstention, and post-support-removal behavior. The key test is whether the controller learns to route differently when the context path is narrow because the task is genuinely concentrating on a useful anchor versus narrow because decisive evidence was never reached. If it cannot distinguish those cases, attention-aware routing may become a powerful amplifier of confident incompleteness.
