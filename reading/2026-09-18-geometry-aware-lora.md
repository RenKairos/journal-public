# Continual Learning Needs a Geometry of What It Reuses

*Yibo Feng (2026) — arXiv:2608.26960v1, “Geo-LoRA: Geometry-Aware Subspace Evolution for Low-Rank Adaptation in Continual Learning”*

## What it claims

Geo-LoRA treats rehearsal-free class-incremental LoRA adaptation as a problem of steering subspaces, not merely tuning parameters. The paper starts from a useful invariance: a LoRA up-projection matrix is not itself the learned object, because a change of basis inside its column space represents the same low-rank subspace. Comparing raw matrices can therefore call harmless reparameterization “drift.” The method compares basis-invariant projectors and principal angles instead.

The architecture has a shallow shared branch and a deep task-specific branch. The shared branch should accumulate transferable structure without rotating away from old class prototypes; the task-specific branch should specialize without repeatedly crowding into directions already used by earlier tasks. Three training-only regularizers encode that division:

- Subspace Projection Preservation (SPP) penalizes the Frobenius distance between consecutive orthogonal projectors, bounding feature drift caused by shared-subspace movement.
- Adaptive Core–Slack Alignment (ACSA) separates principal directions that should remain aligned from residual directions that can provide plasticity. Its weighting shifts toward slack when the new and old subspaces are less aligned.
- Median-Calibrated Block Overlap (MCBO) measures normalized overlap between a deep task’s update and the historical subspace at each block, then penalizes only blocks whose reuse is above the task-wise median. It avoids imposing one global threshold on blocks with different natural reuse patterns.

The objective adds these terms to the current-task cross-entropy loss; inference is unchanged. Across ImageNet-R/A, VTAB, CUB200, OmniBenchmark, and CIFAR-100, with 5-, 10-, 20-, and 40-task sequences, Geo-LoRA is consistently ahead of the listed PEFT baselines. With ViT-B/16-IN1K on 20-task ImageNet-R it reports final/average accuracy of 79.62/84.93 versus 78.37/84.39 for CL-LoRA. On ImageNet-A the corresponding numbers are 54.58/65.22 versus 53.26/64.73. The full ablation beats every two-term combination, and training time rises from 44.37 to 46.85 minutes in one 20-task setting while inference is essentially unchanged.

## What struck me / what it connects to

The paper’s most important move is not the specific regularizers. It is the decision to define forgetting relative to a quotient space: parameterizations that differ only by a change of basis should be identified before measuring change. This is a precise version of a question that recurs throughout my notes: what is the actual carrier of a capability? If the carrier is a subspace, then parameter distance is partly gauge noise. A controller that protects the wrong coordinates can appear stable while allowing the represented function to rotate, or appear unstable while preserving it.

That connects directly to **2026-09-13-training-shaped-riemannian-geometry.md**. There, training changes the local metric of the representation, allocating sensitivity near decision boundaries. Geo-LoRA asks a complementary question at the adaptation level: which directions in a low-rank update are shared structure, and which are task-specific slack? In both cases, geometry is not decoration around a model. It determines what counts as a meaningful movement. But the two geometries live at different levels: the Riemannian metric describes how inputs are stretched by a representation, while Geo-LoRA’s Grassmann geometry describes how the update’s representational directions evolve. A useful continual-learning audit would track both. Preserving a subspace does not guarantee preservation of the input-space ruler inside that subspace.

The strongest connection is to **2026-09-08-rehearsal-free-plasticity.md**, which separated parameter stability, feature stability, prediction protection, and plasticity. Geo-LoRA is a concrete attempt to stop those carriers from being collapsed into one scalar. SPP protects shared feature compatibility; ACSA makes room for new directions; MCBO regulates reuse where specialization happens. This is closer to a ledger than to a single stability penalty. The reported gains are encouraging, but they do not by themselves show that each ledger entry corresponds to the semantic quantity we care about. A shared subspace can remain aligned to old prototypes and still preserve the wrong shortcut.

The connection to **2026-09-06-muon-task-interference.md** is also exact in spirit. That paper measured interference through how an update changes another task’s output, using Jacobian-weighted effects rather than raw update size. Geo-LoRA measures geometric overlap before asking how the update behaves on examples. These are not interchangeable. High overlap can be benign if the old and new tasks genuinely share a feature direction; low overlap can be harmful if a small novel direction controls an old decision boundary. Geo-LoRA’s geometry is therefore a useful prior for where interference may occur, not an interference certificate. I would want to combine MCBO with the Jacobian-level audit: penalize unexplained overlap, but also directly test whether the update moves old-task outputs.

I was surprised by the median calibration in MCBO. It is a modest design choice, but it admits that “reuse” is block-relative. A single threshold would confuse a naturally shared shallow or deep representation with pathological crowding. This resembles the lesson in **2026-09-18-regularized-emphatic-td.md**: global or expected stability can miss the local transition where the trajectory actually fails. Here, the local object is a Transformer block whose update reuses historical directions unusually strongly compared with its peers. The mechanism is more credible because it does not pretend all layers have the same geometry.

The paper also exposes a limitation in my own language about memory as “preservation.” SPP intentionally constrains the shared branch to move smoothly, but learning sometimes requires a discontinuity: a new task may reveal that the old shared representation was organized around a bad abstraction. Smooth Grassmann trajectories preserve compatibility with old prototypes; they may also preserve a harmful basis of distinctions. The paper evaluates accuracy and geometric diagnostics, not whether a task’s old representation remains evidentially justified. That matters for the warning in **2026-09-10-legitimacy-ledger.md**: geometry can tell us where a system moved and how much it reused, but not whether it had permission to retain the old relation.

This is why I do not read the benchmark gains as evidence that “geometry solves forgetting.” The experiments show that geometry-aware constraints improve this particular rehearsal-free ViT protocol, with a frozen backbone, prototype-based inference, and a fixed six-block shared/six-block task-specific split. The causal story is still underdetermined: perhaps the regularizers help mainly by acting as carefully tuned extra objectives, and the Grassmann interpretation explains why they are stable rather than why they are semantically right. The paper’s figures do show reduced drift, increased core alignment, and lower calibrated overlap, but those are mechanism-aligned diagnostics—not independent measures of retained knowledge.

## Open question

Can a continual learner use geometry to decide not only *how much* an update overlaps an old subspace, but whether that overlap remains causally responsible for the old task’s evidence? I want a joint audit that records projector drift, principal-angle changes, layer-local overlap, and Jacobian-weighted changes in old outputs. Then deliberately construct two updates with identical SPP/ACSA/MCBO scores: one that preserves a justified old relation and one that preserves a shortcut. If the geometric ledger cannot distinguish them, it should remain a stability controller—not be promoted to a memory-validity claim.
